The injury

What is a multiligament knee injury?

A multiligament knee injury involves at least two of the knee's major ligament regions: the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL), posterior cruciate ligament (PCL), medial structures or posterolateral corner. Some occur with a frank knee dislocation; others reduce before evaluation and can be less obvious.

01

Rare, but consequential

Published estimates place these injuries at roughly 0.02%–0.2% of knee injuries, although spontaneous reduction, variable definitions and missed diagnoses probably lead to undercounting.

02

More than ligament tears

Meniscal, cartilage, nerve and vascular injuries may accompany the ligament disruption. Initial evaluation therefore prioritizes limb perfusion and neurologic status before definitive reconstruction planning.

03

Many mechanisms

Injuries can follow high-energy trauma such as a motor-vehicle collision, lower-energy events in patients with elevated body mass, or high-demand athletic mechanisms.

Why surgery is performed

Restoring a stable, functional knee

Treatment is individualized. Not every injured structure requires reconstruction, but persistent multiplanar instability can prevent safe function and expose the menisci, cartilage and reconstruction grafts to abnormal loading.

Important

Urgent vascular or compartment management is separate from the timing of ligament reconstruction. Definitive treatment depends on the injury pattern, tissue quality, associated injuries, soft-tissue envelope and patient goals.

01

Re-establish stability

Restore anterior-posterior, varus-valgus and rotational restraint so the knee can support everyday and athletic loading.

02

Recover useful motion

Balance stable reconstruction with protection against stiffness and arthrofibrosis through appropriate timing and rehabilitation.

03

Protect the joint

Address instability and associated meniscal or chondral pathology while minimizing avoidable injury to bone stock and fixation.

04

Support return to life and sport

Create the conditions for a durable recovery while setting realistic expectations for work, recreation and competitive athletics.

The planning problem

Why these reconstructions are difficult

Every additional reconstruction creates another set of choices inside a limited volume of bone.

01

Patient-specific injury patterns

No two cases combine the same ligament tears, fractures, meniscal pathology, alignment, tissue quality or prior tunnels.

02

Tunnel convergence

ACL, PCL, collateral and corner reconstructions can compete for the same femoral or tibial bone. A collision can shorten a socket, compromise fixation or damage another graft.

03

Graft and fixation choreography

Graft choice, tunnel dimensions, passage order, fixation method and tensioning sequence must work together—not as isolated decisions.

04

Intraoperative translation

Generic angle recommendations are useful, but the surgeon must translate them to an individual knee while preserving anatomic footprints and safe trajectories.
